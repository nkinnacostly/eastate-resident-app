How the gate works today

Most estate gates still run on phone calls.

A visitor pulls up and names a house. The guard rings the resident, or the visitor rings their host from the gate, and everyone waits for someone to pick up. It is slow when it works, and a guess when it does not.

01

The call goes unanswered

The resident is in a meeting, on silent or out of battery. The visitor waits at the gate, or the guard decides on their own.

02

A house number proves nothing

Anyone can say they are visiting house 14. Nothing ties the person at the gate to a visit the resident actually expected.

03

Nobody can say who came in

After an incident there is no record of who was let in, by which guard, or on whose word.

How it works

Three steps, about twenty seconds.

The resident sends a code

One tap in the app makes a short code — no look-alike letters, so it survives being read aloud. They forward it on WhatsApp, by SMS or in a call.

The visitor reads it out

Nothing to install and no account to create. At the gate, the code is the whole conversation.

The guard checks it

The guard types it in and sees the house: admit, or refuse with the reason. The code is spent on the spot, and the resident's phone tells them their visitor has arrived.

Estate dues

Dues collected without going house to house.

The estate office sets the dues once. Residents and landlords pay online, the money lands in the estate's account, and the office can see at a glance which houses are settled.

Pay in the app or by link

Residents pay in the app. Landlords pay from an emailed link, with no account needed. A month at a time, or up to a year ahead.

Paid into the estate's account

Payments go through Flutterwave and settle straight into the estate's own bank account. The money never passes through ours.

Arrears, handled fairly

Reminders go out on their own. If the estate switches it on, a house in arrears cannot create new visitor codes until it pays — residents keep their own access, and codes already sent keep working.

Estates, houses, residents

Every house has its own code, so people land in the right home.

An estate manager adds a house and its landlord once. The house gets a code of its own. A resident signs up with the estate code and their house code together, and an administrator approves them before anything can be generated.

Several people share one home — partners, adult children, a tenant — each on their own phone. The second person joins with the same house code, and the guard sees the house name on every verification.

Privacy by construction

A resident's code stays inside the estate.

The estate office can see its own codes and entries, so it can answer a question or cancel a code. Nobody outside the estate can.

That separation is enforced in the database rather than in the interface: each estate's records are walled off from every other estate's, and the platform's own dashboard is built never to return a code. A guard sees a host's name only when a valid code is checked, and the gate phone holds no names at all.

Questions

Frequently asked

How does a visitor code work?

A resident opens the app and generates a short code, then forwards it to their visitor by WhatsApp, SMS or a phone call. At the gate the guard types it in and the app either admits the visitor or refuses, telling the guard exactly why. Each code works once and expires after six hours.

Does the guard still need to call the resident?

Not for a visitor with a code. The code is the resident's say-so, given in advance, so the guard checks it instead of phoning the house. The resident gets a notification the moment it is used.

What happens if the gate has no internet?

The guard app keeps a local copy of the estate's live codes on the phone, so verification carries on with no signal at all. Entries are queued and synced the moment the connection returns. A network outage never becomes a gate outage.

Can a code be used twice?

No. Codes are single use. The moment one is accepted it is spent, and a second attempt is refused and recorded as a double-entry attempt for the estate manager to review.

How do residents join an estate?

Every estate has a code and every house within it has its own. A resident signs up with both, which places them in the right home, and an estate administrator approves them before any code can be generated. Nobody adds themselves to an estate.

How do estate dues work?

The estate office sets the dues and who pays them: each resident, or the landlord of each house. Residents pay in the app and landlords from an emailed link, for a month or up to a year ahead, and the money settles into the estate's own bank account. If the estate switches it on, a house in arrears cannot create new visitor codes until it pays; residents keep their own access, and codes already sent keep working.

Who can see a resident's visitor codes?

The resident who created them, the guard they are presented to, and the administrators of that estate, who can see their own estate's codes and entries so they can answer questions and cancel a code. Nobody at any other estate can, and the platform's own dashboard never shows a code.

Do visitors or guards need anything new?

Visitors install nothing and create no account; they receive a short code through whatever messaging app the resident already uses. Guards use the guard app on an ordinary Android phone. There are no turnstiles, scanners or barrier controllers to buy.

What happens when a resident moves out?

The estate manager deactivates their membership. Their account stays intact — people move between estates — but they can no longer generate codes for the home they left, and any live codes are revoked.
